The model answers whatever you hand it.

A propensity model will fit on any set of columns and return a lift number with a confidence interval. Whether that number means anything was decided earlier, by what you asked and what you let into the model.

Most of the habits that make a good targeting model make a bad propensity analysis. The code looks the same, which is why the habits carry over unnoticed.

A raw difference is two numbers added together.

Exposed respondents differ from controls for two reasons. The campaign changed them, and they were different people to begin with. The raw gap cannot tell these apart.

Exposed − controlwhat the survey shows
=
Effect on the exposedwhat the campaign did (the ATT)
+
Selection biashow the two groups would have differed with no campaign at all

Random assignment makes the second term zero by construction. Without it, adjustment can only remove the part of selection bias that runs through variables you measured.

    Four kinds of variable. Four different answers.

    Include

    Causes of both exposure and the outcome.

    Age, media habits, prior relationship with the brand, category involvement. These are the confounders, and the analysis exists to balance them. Be generous here: leaving one out biases the estimate, while including a weak one costs little.

    Include if you have it

    Causes of the outcome only.

    They do not predict exposure, so they barely change the scores. They still tighten the estimate, because they soak up outcome variation that would otherwise be noise.

    Leave out

    Causes of exposure only.

    Targeting flags, ad-server quirks, which panel a respondent came from. They raise the AUC, thin out the overlap and widen the interval. If a confounder is still missing, they make its bias larger.

    Never

    Anything the exposure could have changed.

    Ad recall, site visits, post-campaign attitudes, anything measured after the first impression that the ad might have moved. Adjusting for it removes real effect or manufactures a false one.

    The survey ran after the campaign.

    Every answer in a post-campaign survey was given after exposure. “Prior brand usage” asked today is a memory, and seeing the ad can change what people remember. Treat a covariate as pre-exposure only when exposure could not plausibly have altered the answer: demographics, long-run media habits, purchases dated before the flight.

    Do not read the model's feature importance as a finding.

    The importance chart describes who got exposed. It says nothing about what drives awareness or intent, and the coefficients of adjustment variables are not effects of those variables. Epidemiologists call this the Table 2 fallacy.

    Decide before you look at lift.

    Choosing features, a caliper, a trimming rule and a method is design work, and it can all be done without opening the outcome columns. Tune those choices while watching the lift number and you are searching for a result. Five yes-or-no choices already make 32 different analyses.

    1. Write the question down.

      Population, exposure, comparison, outcome. One sentence, agreed with whoever asked for the number.

    2. Describe how people came to be exposed.

      Who decided who saw the ad: targeting rules, placements, the respondent's own media habits. This is the list your covariates have to cover.

    3. List the common causes, then check when each was fixed.

      For every driver of exposure, ask whether it also moves the outcome, whether you measured it, and whether exposure could have changed the answer.

    4. Name what you could not measure.

      There is always something. Writing it down tells readers which direction the estimate is likely to be wrong in.

    5. Fix the estimand, the methods, the trimming rule and the primary KPI.

      Run several methods and report all of them. Agreement between methods is reassuring; picking the best-looking one is not.

    6. Check overlap and balance. Then open the lift results.

      If the design fails those checks, change the design. Do it before the outcomes are in view.
