Frame first. Then model.

propensity_lift estimates brand lift from exposed-versus-control survey data. This guide covers the package, and the decisions that have to be made before you run it.

Start with the comparison everyone makes.

In this simulated survey, exposed respondents are more aware of the brand than controls by +19 points. It is tempting to call that lift.

The groups were never alike.

Heavy media users were likelier to see the ad, and likelier to know the brand already. Part of the gap existed before the campaign ran.

A propensity score puts everyone on one axis.

It is each respondent's estimated chance of exposure, given what you measured. Where both colours appear, a like-for-like comparison exists.

Reweight controls to resemble the exposed.

Controls who look like exposed respondents now count for more. The gap falls from +19 to +8 points. The other 11 were confounding.

That was the easy part.

It worked because the simulation's only confounder was in the model. With real data you have to argue that, and nothing in the output can confirm it.

A propensity model does not know what question you asked.

If you build response or targeting models, the mechanics here are familiar: a classifier, a score, some weights. What changes is the job. A targeting model succeeds by predicting exposure well. A propensity analysis succeeds when the comparison it sets up is fair, and a model that predicts exposure too well is a sign that no fair comparison exists.

Three things the output cannot tell you.

The package reports balance, overlap and confidence intervals. All three can look excellent while the answer is wrong, because each of these is settled outside the data.

You measured why people were exposed.

Every cause of exposure that also moves the outcome is in the model. This is unconfoundedness, and it cannot be tested.

No unmeasured confounding

Comparable people exist.

For every kind of exposed respondent there are controls like them. This is overlap, and the package can show you where it fails.

Overlap and positivity

“Exposed” means one thing.

One well-defined exposure, with no spillover from one respondent to another. This is consistency, and it is decided when you code the flag.

A well-defined exposure

When observational ad measurement was checked against experiments, it missed badly.

These studies had far richer data than a survey and still went wrong, because the variables that drove exposure were not in the data. The estimator was rarely the weak point.

160×

Lewis, Rao and Reiley (2011). A randomised test measured a 5.4% effect on brand searches. Regression with every available control said 872%.

7 of 14

Gordon et al. (2019). In seven of the 14 Facebook experiments with a checkout outcome, the observational estimates were off by more than a factor of three.

173% vs 29%

Gordon, Moakler and Zettelmeyer (2023). Over 663 experiments, the median upper-funnel lift from stratified propensity score matching, against the median lift the experiments measured.

One call runs the whole analysis.

Give it a respondent-level table and a config. It fits the propensity model, checks overlap, applies every adjustment method you asked for, and reports lift for each KPI with diagnostics next to every number.

Five estimators, side by side.

Nearest-neighbour matching, inverse probability weighting, overlap weights, stratification and doubly robust AIPW run from one config. When they disagree, that is information.

Reading overlap

Balance, before and after.

Standardised mean differences for every covariate, with the same denominator on both sides so the two are comparable.

Balance diagnostics

Intervals that include the adjustment.

The bootstrap redoes the matching or weighting on every resample, so the interval reflects the adjustment step and not only the final difference in means.
